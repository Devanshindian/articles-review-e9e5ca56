Run a Hiring Hackathon: Hackathon Judging & AI Policy

A hackathon without a published judging rubric and a clear AI-use policy selects for demo polish and hidden tool use, not job-readiness. The fix is a weighted hackathon judging rubric with anchored scores and explicit rules for when and how AI tools may be used, disclosed, and verified. This article provides both, showing how to define must-have competencies, design challenges that surface reasoning, and calibrate judges to produce fair, defensible hiring decisions.

  • A weighted judging rubric with five to seven anchored dimensions forces every judge to score the same evidence, not personal impressions
  • AI-disallowed rules for live rounds and AI-as-tool rules with mandatory disclosure for take-home work prevent submissions that candidates cannot explain.
  • Calibration sessions where judges independently score sample submissions and align on disagreements before the event yield inter-rater reliability of at least r ≥.70.
  • A per-dimension minimum score rule (such as automatic no-hire for a 1 in any critical competency) prevents high scores in other areas from masking a fatal gap.
  • AI detection tools have false-positive rates around 4% and must be used as triggers for review, not as verdicts.

What Is a Hiring Hackathon? How Does a Hackathon Work and Screen for Real Skill?

A hiring hackathon is a structured work-sample event: candidates complete tasks that mirror the job under controlled conditions, and their output is scored against predetermined criteria. The core purpose is prediction: it asks candidates to show what they can do on a bounded slice of the role, rather than describe how they would work. Unlike a generic hackathon, whose main goals are idea generation or publicity, a hiring hackathon’s purpose is evidence of role-related competence. It borrows the event format but applies the same instructions, materials, and rubric to every candidate, turning the work-sample idea into a timed selection exercise. The event sits inside a broader shift from resume-led screening toward evidence-led hiring, where employers observe candidates doing work that resembles the role instead of inferring ability from credentials. The challenge itself becomes the assessment: it tests the skills the employer is hiring for, not a team’s ability to assemble a polished demo. This demands that technical screening move beyond CVs and cover letters by asking candidates to solve role-relevant scenarios that reveal hands-on capability.

Step 1: Define the Role and Core Competencies

Define the competencies the hackathon will measure before you write a single challenge. A job analysis links every scored task to a business need and makes the evidence defensible. Ask what the hire must deliver in the first 90 days and what should be true after 12 months; map those outcomes to competencies like technical execution, debugging depth, or decisions under uncertainty. Separate must-haves (skills without which the person cannot do the job) from differentiators that can raise a score without excluding a qualified candidate.

Tailor the competencies to the specific engineering role, not a generic title. A backend engineer’s must-haves often include RESTful API design, debugging a realistic codebase, or system design, directly tied to responsibilities in the job description. A cloud infrastructure role needs architecture scenarios and CLI troubleshooting, not the same coding test. For any engineering role, account for both hard skills and the soft skills that surface in code quality judgment, collaboration, and product reasoning.

Calibrate seniority by behavior, not by years of experience (a weak proxy) 7. Build a competency matrix: junior developers execute tasks with detailed guidance; mid-level developers execute independently and solve standard problems; senior developers identify problems before they happen, mentor others, and define technical strategy 7. For staff engineers, add technical direction for an organization and keeping projects moving; principal engineers set direction for the company and influence business alignment 6. Title inflation is common, so require evidence of ownership, mentorship, and cross-functional awareness rather than accepting a “Senior” prefix 67. Once competencies and levels are set, the challenge statements can be designed to produce observable evidence at the right bar.

Step 2: Design Realistic Challenge Statements and Choose the Right Assessment Formats

The formats you pick decide whether your hackathon screens for job-relevant skill or for test-taking reflexes. Start by mapping every exercise to a task the role actually performs. A backend engineer should debug a failing service with logs and test harnesses instead of solving an abstract algorithm puzzle. The strongest predictor is a work sample that mirrors the job: configure a network, build a small feature, clean a messy dataset. Weight the challenge toward must-have skills: a 70/30 split (70% daily tasks, 30% adjacent knowledge) keeps scores tied to on-the-job performance, not trivia recall.

One format alone rarely captures enough signal. Combine a coding test that reveals speed and correctness with a live debugging walkthrough that exposes communication and reasoning. Add a scenario walkthrough that asks how the candidate would handle a system failure, and a short collaborative drill if teamwork matters. No single exercise covers every dimension of readiness, so a mix of knowledge checks, hands-on builds, and scenario-based prompts gives a fuller view. Keep the total assessment tight: cap take-home tasks at two to three hours or strong candidates will withdraw.

Avoid proxies that substitute for applied skill. Generic algorithm challenges reward competition-style tricks over the day-to-day coding most roles require, and senior engineers often underperform on them relative to their real job capability. Multiple-choice questions tell you whether a candidate knows the definition of a race condition, not whether they can find one in a codebase. A challenge unrelated to the company’s business sector (a logistics firm asking a candidate to build a phone-bill app) hurts candidate perception and gives weaker evidence of fit. Vague, overly broad problem statements cause teams to spend the first six hours debating scope, producing superficial prototypes that hide real ability. Instead, write tasks that are discrete but representative, so candidates can show partial success even if they do not finish every item.

In the AI era, the final artifact alone no longer proves skill. AI can produce a working solution to a basic coding question in seconds, obscuring whether the candidate framed the problem correctly, evaluated suggestions, or took ownership of the outcome. Design challenges that surface process: provide a repository with messy logs, missing edge cases, or inconsistent naming conventions and ask the candidate to ship a fix, then explain what they’d refactor with more time. Score the reasoning around the code: finding the right place to make a change, evaluating an AI-generated suggestion for correctness and risk, making trade-offs under ambiguity. A live walkthrough where the candidate must explain why they chose one approach over another converts process evidence into a defensible hiring signal. The next step formalizes an AI-use policy, but the challenge itself must already require and capture the reasoning the policy will later demand candidates disclose.

Step 3: Establish a Clear AI-Use Policy for Hackathon Participants

A hiring hackathon needs an explicit AI-use policy because candidates already use tools such as Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot, and Cursor during take-home or async work, while live rounds require a different rule set. Banning AI outright on an asynchronous coding test without a follow-up walk-through is unenforceable; the same is true when a policy contradicts itself: telling candidates “do not use AI” while assigning a task that would take 12 hours without it. The cleanest structure separates three policies by stage: AI-disallowed for live interviews or tightly controlled rounds, AI-as-tool for take-home work samples and async screening, and AI-required when the role itself involves LLM features, prompt writing, or AI-output review.

For live demos, expert-panel reviews, and tightly controlled rounds, state the AI-disallowed policy plainly: no AI tools, no chat windows, and no second monitors for the duration of the round, with candidates expected to get stuck out loud because that is part of the signal. For take-home or async work, the AI-as-tool policy permits any AI tool the candidate would use on the job, provided the final submission is code the candidate can explain, defend, and modify under live questioning. A short README note listing which tools were used and where is the standard format.

Permitted AI assistance covers brainstorming ideas, refining research questions, finding information, drafting an outline, checking grammar or style, code debugging, and pseudocode explanations, all with the condition that the final submission reflects the candidate’s own understanding and effort 4. AI may supplement the work but cannot replace the candidate’s thinking, judgment, or creative labor 4. Prohibited uses include undisclosed generation, submitting AI-generated work as one’s own, impersonating a participant, fabricating citations or data, and any hidden live assistance that substitutes for the candidate’s spontaneous reasoning 4.

When AI assistance is permitted, disclosure is mandatory. Even where AI use is allowed, undisclosed use violates integrity requirements. The disclosure must be specific: name the tool and version if known, describe the exact task it performed, identify the affected sections, and confirm that the candidate independently verified all sources and factual claims. A reusable template: “I used [tool name] for [brainstorming / outlining / grammar review / translation / coding assistance]. AI was used on [specific sections or tasks]. I independently verified all sources and factual claims. The final argument, source selection, and submitted wording are my responsibility.” The disclosure appears in the submission itself, at the end or in a designated form field. Candidates must also preserve process evidence (prompt logs, chat histories, version history, and notes on how outputs were used) because that log protects the author if AI use is questioned and demonstrates transparency. When no AI was used at all, the candidate should state that explicitly 8.

The policy must separate plagiarism from AI assistance. Plagiarism is an ethical offense involving appropriation of another’s work or insights without attribution; it is distinct from copyright infringement, which is a legal wrong 5. A candidate can plagiarize without infringing, and can infringe without plagiarizing 5. For the hackathon, prohibited plagiarism includes copied code, copied text, copied designs, prior work reused without permission, and violation of another person’s or entity’s intellectual property rights. Presenting AI-generated content as one’s own work without authorization and attribution is plagiarism and an integrity violation 4. Even when AI is used, the submission must be the entrant’s original work.

Candidates and organizers must not enter confidential, proprietary, restricted, customer, or personally identifiable information into public AI tools 48. Public AI tools may use inputs for training, and information submitted may be treated as public disclosure 8. If the organization provides approved enterprise-grade AI tools with data protections, candidates should use only those for any restricted materials. Disclosure should note privacy limits when relevant, especially if candidate data or employer-provided materials were involved.

Enforcement relies on staged, proportional sanctions. Minor omissions (a missing disclosure of a grammar check) can be handled with a note and follow-up question. Deliberate fraud, such as submitting an AI-generated full submission, using hidden live assistance, or fabricating citations, leads to score reduction or disqualification. Interviewers should be trained to ask follow-up questions and document what happened rather than ejecting the candidate mid-interview. The policy must be published before registration, included verbatim in candidate-facing emails, and reinforced in a 20-minute calibration session for judges.

Step 4: Construct a Hackathon Judging Rubric with Clear Performance Anchors

With the AI-use policy defined, a hackathon judging rubric turns a roomful of individual opinions into one defensible shortlist. Without one, judges silently weight different things (one rewards speed, another rewards novelty) and the person hired is whoever happened to impress the loudest reviewer. The fix is a weighted rubric with anchored score definitions that every judge applies the same way.

Start by converting the three to five job-critical outcomes from Step 1 into rubric dimensions. Keep the list short: five to seven at most, because too many criteria push evaluators back toward gut instinct. For a backend engineering role, the dimensions might be technical execution, problem-solving, testing and verification, code quality, and communication of decisions. For a product role, weight problem clarity, customer evidence, and business reasoning above demo polish. Every dimension must tie directly to what the person would do on the job.

Assign a percentage weight to each dimension so the scores reflect the role’s priorities. Weights must sum to 100%. If the role demands rigorous debugging under an existing codebase, weight execution correctness and code maintainability more heavily than novelty. Use the formula `weighted points = score × weight` for each dimension.

Define what each score means with concrete behavioral descriptors. A 1 to 5 scale is common practice. Anchor it with observable evidence so two judges reading the same work can see why a candidate received a 2, a 3, or a 5. For example, on technical execution: a 5 means the solution is functional, efficient, well-tested, and the candidate discussed tradeoffs. A 3 means it works and is complete. A 1 means the core deliverables are non-functional or the approach is incorrect. Avoid vague labels like “good” or “poor”: they mean different things to different evaluators and make scoring inconsistent.

Build a scorecard that captures the number and the evidence behind it. Every judge writes down the specific artifact, quote, or observable action that justified the rating. Evidence rules should state what reviewers may use (only the source material and the demo, or outside knowledge) because clear rules reduce disagreement. A “Not enough evidence” option prevents judges from guessing when the proof is missing.

Set decision rules before anyone scores. Define the total weighted score that triggers an advance, and write a per-dimension rule for a must-have competency that cannot be compensated by a high score elsewhere. For example, a score of 1 in any critical dimension might be an automatic no-hire regardless of other scores. Decide how to handle borderline cases: two 2s might require a calibration conversation, while a 5 identifies candidates to fast-track. Specify tie-break logic in advance: one model compares tied submissions on the highest score in the first weighted criterion, moving to the next if the tie persists.

In an AI-permitted hackathon, the rubric must separate high-quality process from polished output. Add a dimension for AI-assisted assessment that scores how candidates prompted, evaluated, tested, and corrected AI output rather than whether they produced a working answer. Observable signals include scoping prompts with appropriate context, recognizing when AI output is incomplete or incorrect, and modifying generated code to fit system constraints. A red flag is uncritical acceptance of AI output, which rubrics can define and penalize explicitly. This prevents the hackathon from rewarding a candidate who pastes in generated code they cannot explain.

If the event is team-based, add an individual contribution layer. Raw commit counts and lines changed are weak proxies for contribution and should not carry heavy weight alone. Pair them with evidence from code reviews, issue tracker participation, and the candidate’s explanation of their own work during the live demo. Observers can record how teams responded to mentor input and made trade-offs while work was still forming. The strongest signal often comes from the panel questioning: a candidate who can walk through architecture choices, identify edge cases, and defend what they shipped demonstrates ownership that static artifacts cannot prove.

Step 5: Select and Calibrate Judges for Consistent, Unbiased Evaluation

A rubric is only half the job. Even a well-built scoring framework fails when judges interpret score bands differently or default to personal criteria. The difference between a defensible hire and a shortlist shaped by reviewer preference comes down to who judges, how they are prepared, and the procedural guardrails that keep evaluation consistent.

Start by assembling a panel that maps to the competencies the hackathon tests. A credible judging team combines deep domain knowledge with varied perspectives (technology, business, design, and sector-specific backgrounds) so no submission gets judged through a single lens. Technical judges assess systems thinking, debugging depth, and code quality; business or stakeholder-oriented evaluators weigh communication, ownership, and customer judgment. Do not select purely by seniority. A senior leader who has not built anything in a decade often rewards stories, while active builders reward proof. Mixing both prevents the panel from rewarding only polish or business storytelling.

Calibration happens before the event, never during it. Schedule a 60- to 90-minute session the week prior, walking the full panel through the rubric and having every judge score two to three sample submissions independently. Compare disagreements afterward, focusing first on large score gaps and repeated patterns, not isolated misses. When judges consistently disagree on the same criterion, the problem is usually ambiguous rubric anchors, not the judges. Fix the rubric by adding clearer behavioral descriptors, defining edge cases, and tightening evidence rules, and then retest alignment.

Procedural safeguards hold the line on event day. Independent scoring, where each judge enters ratings without seeing colleagues’ scores, reduces anchoring bias. Blinded review, removing names, institutions, and demographic markers, helps judges evaluate the work, not the person. Randomly assign projects to judges, require conflict-of-interest disclosures, and instruct evaluators to focus solely on each project’s merits.

Close with a quantitative check. Multiple evaluators should score independently, and their agreement should reach at least r ≥.70 for the process to be reliable. Finally, preserve an audit trail: a written record of every score, the evidence behind it, and the rationale for any tied or escalated decision. That documentation transforms subjective judgment into a repeatable, defensible selection process.

Step 6: Plan Event Logistics, Participant Communications, and Mentoring

With the hackathon judging panel calibrated, the focus shifts to planning the event itself. Start by working backward from the event end date; allow four to eight weeks of planning for a hackathon that runs one to three days. Define the theme and core stakeholders: mentors, judges, participants, organizers. Then lock the rules, team size, and timeline before launch.

Registration must collect role-relevant skill data and include explicit acknowledgement of the AI-use and judging policies. Form cross-functional teams of two to five people with a frontend developer, a backend builder, and someone who owns the pitch; a matchmaking tool or dedicated Discord channel helps participants self-assemble.

Send pre-reads before work begins that cover the problem statements, rubric, and agenda. A message library of kickoff scripts, announcements, and reminders keeps communication consistent. Build employer brand with executive talks, mentoring sessions, and professional follow-up with every participant.

Schedule mentor checkpoints during the build to kill weak ideas early and give real-time feedback; mentors rotate in person or use dedicated channels in virtual settings, briefed on objectives and guidelines beforehand.

The run-of-show is minute-by-minute: kickoff rituals, mentor checkpoints, pitch coaching, final demos, and live judging. For onsite events, seat teams at banquet-style tables; for online events, use a platform that supports submission collection, participant monitoring, and evaluation.

Choose a format deliberately. Virtual events reach the widest, most diverse pool at the lowest cost, while in-person events provide a deeper face-to-face read on candidates. Hybrid formats combine both but require demo links that are accessible to all judges and scoring that does not tilt toward room presence.

Finally, match the challenge environment to real work. Let candidates use their own IDE and avoid browser-only coding unless the job requires it; prefer a time suggestion over a hard cutoff so they write production-quality code.

Step 7: Run the Hackathon: Submissions, Live Demos, and Real-Time Scoring

The submission window closes at a hard deadline: one hackathon rule froze code on Sunday at 17:00. Every team must submit a complete evidence package: a working demo, a README with setup instructions and tech stack, significant build-period development, and, where required, AI/ML as a core component. A compliant technical submission also includes a text project summary, a demonstration video of around three minutes, at least one test script, and screenshots of test results and platform features. The video must show the project functioning on its intended device, be uploaded publicly, and avoid unlicensed copyrighted material. The project must be accessible for judging and testing (free of charge until the judging period ends) via a website, functioning demo, or test build, with private credentials supplied if needed. After the deadline, changes are barred except for narrow fixes such as removing PII or infringing material, and the modified submission must stay substantively the same.

Online and in-person formats each shape how judges collect evidence. Online, automated test execution can validate functionality, run unit and integration tests in sandboxed environments, and flag deviations. In-person and hybrid events let mentors and judges capture process evidence during checkpoints, rehearsals, and demos, not just final output. Regardless of format, live demos use the same rubric anchors as remote review so presentation charisma does not replace job-relevant evidence. A typical structure gives each team five minutes: three for the presentation and two for questions, producing both a working artifact and an explanation window.

Automated scoring speeds consistency, but it cannot replace human judgment. Test execution can confirm functional correctness, while static analysis and documentation checks can score code quality and completeness. Yet human review is needed because purely software-driven assessments miss nuance in code quality, intention, and refactoring ability. The strongest approach combines automated scoring with human review, especially for mid-to-senior roles where architecture and reasoning matter. After hackathon judging, the panel preserves rubric category scores, reviewer notes, and any written rationales for score corrections, creating an audit trail and making debriefs consistent.

Integrity checks (AI detection, plagiarism screening, and code similarity analysis) are triage signals, not verdicts. AI-detection tools can produce false positives; a 4% false-positive rate can mean thousands of inaccurate flags across a large candidate pool. Plagiarism tools compare submissions against known sources and flag highly similar work, but a flagged report is a trigger for review, not proof of misconduct. Detector scores should prompt a process review, a draft request, or an oral explanation, never an automatic disqualification. Before any penalty, gather multiple evidence sources: the AI disclosure statement, commit history, similarity report, live code explanation, and mentor observations. Document the concern, review the evidence against the published policy, and let the candidate explain before a finding is made.

Live demos and Q&A sessions also surface hidden AI assistance. Warning signs include large blocks of code appearing instantly, inconsistent indentation, and a candidate who cannot explain edge cases or testing strategies 3. Invisible overlay tools can hide AI copilots from screen share, but network-layer enforcement can block their API calls 9. Identity fraud checks (face-presence verification and identity-continuity signals) address proxy test-takers. Code similarity tools like MOSS use token-based and AST analysis to detect structural matches beyond surface-level text comparison, but the tool’s creators caution that it is not a fully automatic plagiarism detector and cannot judge intent. Similarity alone does not prove cheating; human review is required.

When a candidate submits code they cannot explain, or produces a complete solution with no visible reasoning or iteration, those are signals that the work may not be their own 3. Engineers rarely write perfect code in one pass; they start rough, iterate, and refine 3. A mismatch between the submitted solution and the candidate’s live explanation justifies a deeper review and additional questioning, not an immediate penalty 3. The process keeps the final hiring decision grounded in verified skill, not a single detector flag.

Step 8: Follow Up, Extend Offers, and Refine Your Hackathon Process

Move fast from hackathon scores to offers. Separate winners, finalists, and hire/no-hire recommendations and share the hiring pathway before registration opens. Follow up within days with named contacts and concrete next steps; waiting weeks can lose top prospects to competing offers. Engaged participants you do not hire can become advocates and a standing pipeline if the experience was respectful. Close the loop with winner announcements, a 60-minute debrief that covers outcomes, ROI, and next-iteration design.

Convert the hackathon evidence into structured interviews instead of unstructured post-hoc chats. Unstructured interviews lack a fixed question set and a scoring rubric, which exposes decisions to bias and limits validity. Structured interviews ask every candidate the same job-related questions and score answers against an anchored rubric, yielding higher interrater reliability and better predictive validity (0.44 versus 0.33 for unstructured) 1. Build a scorecard with behavioral anchors for each rating level 2 and use behavioral and situational questions. Train interviewers on the rubric and calibrate regularly by reviewing sample answers together; mock interviews with current team members test questions and grading before live use.

Match the follow-up interview format to the role. Live coding suits algorithmic and junior-to-mid positions, system design fits senior and staff engineers, pair programming works when collaboration is central, and take-home reviews serve roles where production-quality code counts most [c4033-c4036]. For every format, score communication, problem solving, and collaboration on explicit scales. A communication rubric moves from “cannot communicate clearly” to “organised, succinct, and makes the thought process easy to follow”; problem-solving ranges from no solution to a well-thought-out answer with trade-offs discussed; collaboration runs from dismissive to “makes the interviewer want to work with the candidate”. Give soft skills explicit weight where job-relevant.

Pilot and measure continuously. Before the first event, run the rubric and tasks with two or three strong internal performers to catch timing problems and ambiguous instructions. After each hackathon, track completion rates, score distributions, and post-hire performance correlations quarterly. A/B test the new screen against the prior process with at least 30 to 50 hires per arm, comparing interview load, offer rates, and early performance. Set pass thresholds conservatively at first and tighten them as outcome data accumulates. Monitor per-stage funnel selection rates by subgroup to locate disparities early; teams often find gaps at the resume screen or assessment stage, not at the offer 2. Remove items that show differential performance by subgroup.

After the event, hold a retrospective with judges, sponsors, and organizers to review the experience, rubric effectiveness, and process gaps. Gather participant feedback on the assessment experience. Refine challenges, rubrics, and AI policy based on the findings, and review benchmarks annually so tasks stay aligned with current team practices. Treat each hackathon as one cycle in an improving system: the data from this event shapes a more predictive next event.

What should we do if judges disagree strongly on a score? added by the editor

First, compare disagreements after calibrated practice sessions and fix rubric anchors that cause consistent gaps. Define the tie-break logic before anyone scores. One reliable model compares the tied submissions on the first weighted criterion; if that score is identical, move to the next criterion in order of weight until the tie resolves.

In an AI-permitted hackathon, the rubric must separate high-quality process from polished output [c2918]. Add a dimension for AI-assisted assessment that scores how candidates prompted, evaluated, tested, and corrected AI output rather than whether they produced a working answer [c1376][c1377]. Observable signals include scoping prompts with appropriate context, recognizing when AI output is incomplete or incorrect, and modifying generated code to fit system constraints [c1424][c1425]. A red flag is uncritical acceptance of AI output, which rubrics can define and penalize explicitly [c2327][c3956]. This prevents the hackathon from rewarding a candidate who pastes in generated code they cannot explain [c4254]. If the event is team-based, add an individual contribution layer. Raw commit counts and lines changed are weak proxies for contribution and should not carry heavy weight alone [c2981][c2982]. Pair them with evidence from code reviews, issue tracker participation, and the candidate’s explanation of their own work during the live demo [c3646][c3647]. Observers can record how teams responded to mentor input and made trade-offs while work was still forming [c3294][c3296]. The strongest signal often comes from the panel questioning: a candidate who can walk through architecture choices, identify edge cases, and defend what they shipped demonstrates ownership that static artifacts cannot prove [c3582][c3656].

With the hackathon judging panel calibrated, the focus shifts to planning the event itself. Start by working backward from the event end date; allow four to eight weeks of planning for a hackathon that runs one to three days [c24, c185]. Define the theme and core stakeholders—mentors, judges, participants, organizers—then lock the rules, team size, and timeline before launch [c89, c2596]. Registration must collect role-relevant skill data and include explicit acknowledgement of the AI-use and judging policies [c2312, c2592]. Form cross‑functional teams of two to five people with a frontend developer, a backend builder, and someone who owns the pitch; a matchmaking tool or dedicated Discord channel helps participants self‑assemble [c248, c2313]. Send pre‑reads before work begins that cover the problem statements, rubric, and agenda [c855]. A message library of kickoff scripts, announcements, and reminders keeps communication consistent [c858]. Build employer brand with executive talks, mentoring sessions, and professional follow‑up with every participant [c219]. Schedule mentor checkpoints during the build to kill weak ideas early and give real‑time feedback; mentors rotate in person or use dedicated channels in virtual settings, briefed on objectives and guidelines beforehand [c706, c704, c2319, c3308]. The run‑of‑show is minute‑by‑minute: kickoff rituals, mentor checkpoints, pitch coaching, final demos, and live judging [c853]. For onsite events, seat teams at banquet‑style tables; for online events, use a platform that supports submission collection, participant monitoring, and evaluation [c838, c873]. Choose a format deliberately. Virtual events reach the widest, most diverse pool at the lowest cost, while in‑person events provide a deeper face‑to‑face read on candidates. Hybrid formats combine both but require demo links that are accessible to all judges and scoring that does not tilt toward room presence [c85, c3808, c3592, c3593]. Finally, match the challenge environment to real work. Let candidates use their own IDE and avoid browser‑only coding unless the job requires it; prefer a time suggestion over a hard cutoff so they write production‑quality code [c507, c867, c871].
Step 7: Run the Hackathon: Submissions, Live Demos, and Real‑Time Scoring declared

The submission window closes at a hard deadline — one hackathon rule froze code on Sunday at 17:00 [c2572]. Every team must submit a complete evidence package: a working demo, a README with setup instructions and tech stack, significant build-period development, and, where required, AI/ML as a core component [c2573]. A compliant technical submission also includes a text project summary, a demonstration video of around three minutes, at least one test script, and screenshots of test results and platform features [c2228]. The video must show the project functioning on its intended device, be uploaded publicly, and avoid unlicensed copyrighted material [c2229]. The project must be accessible for judging and testing — free of charge until the judging period ends — via a website, functioning demo, or test build, with private credentials supplied if needed [c2234][c2235]. After the deadline, changes are barred except for narrow fixes such as removing PII or infringing material, and the modified submission must stay substantively the same [c2106]. Online and in-person formats each shape how judges collect evidence. Online, automated test execution can validate functionality, run unit and integration tests in sandboxed environments, and flag deviations [c3791]. In-person and hybrid events let mentors and judges capture process evidence during checkpoints, rehearsals, and demos — not just final output [c3780][c3781]. Regardless of format, live demos use the same rubric anchors as remote review so presentation charisma does not replace job-relevant evidence [c3037]. A typical structure gives each team five minutes: three for the presentation and two for questions, producing both a working artifact and an explanation window [c2574]. Automated scoring speeds consistency, but it cannot replace human judgment. Test execution can confirm functional correctness, while static analysis and documentation checks can score code quality and completeness [c3791][c3795]. Yet human review is needed because purely software-driven assessments miss nuance in code quality, intention, and refactoring ability [c347][c350]. The strongest approach combines automated scoring with human review, especially for mid-to-senior roles where architecture and reasoning matter [c3494][c270]. After scoring, hackathon judging, the panel preserves rubric category scores, reviewer notes, and any written rationales for score corrections, creating an audit trail and making debriefs consistent [c3245][c3246]. Integrity checks — AI detection, plagiarism screening, and code similarity analysis — are triage signals, not verdicts. AI-detection tools can produce false positives; a 4% false-positive rate can mean thousands of inaccurate flags across a large candidate pool [c2378][c2379]. Plagiarism tools compare submissions against known sources and flag highly similar work, but a flagged report is a trigger for review, not proof of misconduct [c1463][c1355]. Detector scores should prompt a process review, a draft request, or an oral explanation — never an automatic disqualification [c2126]. Before any penalty, gather multiple evidence sources: the AI disclosure statement, commit history, similarity report, live code explanation, and mentor observations [c1410]. Document the concern, review the evidence against the published policy, and let the candidate explain before a finding is made [c2099][c2132]. Live demos and Q&A sessions also surface hidden AI assistance. Warning signs include large blocks of code appearing instantly, inconsistent indentation, and a candidate who cannot explain edge cases or testing strategies [c1335][c1336]. Invisible overlay tools can hide AI copilots from screen share, but network-layer enforcement can block their API calls [c1340][c1342]. Identity fraud checks — face-presence verification and identity-continuity signals — address proxy test-takers [c4093]. Code similarity tools like MOSS use token-based and AST analysis to detect structural matches beyond surface-level text comparison, but the tool’s creators caution that it is not a fully automatic plagiarism detector and cannot judge intent [c2925][c3373]. Similarity alone does not prove cheating; human review is required [c3411]. When a candidate submits code they cannot explain, or produces a complete solution with no visible reasoning or iteration, those are signals that the work may not be their own [c1719][c1721]. Engineers rarely write perfect code in one pass; they start rough, iterate, and refine [c1723]. A mismatch between the submitted solution and the candidate’s live explanation justifies a deeper review and additional questioning, not an immediate penalty [c1719]. The process keeps the final hiring decision grounded in verified skill, not a single detector flag.
