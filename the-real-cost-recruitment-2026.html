The $4,700 Era Is Over, SHRM's 2025 Data Shows Cost-Per-Hire Jumped 21% to $5,475

SHRM’s 2025 Benchmarking Survey, fielded January 9 to March 3, 2025 with 2,371 member respondents, now places the U.S. average at $5,475 for non-executive roles and $35,879 for executive roles, a 21% increase from its prior benchmark. The earlier $4,700 figure reflected a previous SHRM benchmarking cycle that has been explicitly superseded 1. SHRM attributes the rise to increasing recruiter compensation, greater sourcing complexity, and shifting job-board pricing models. The move is not an outlier; cost-per-hire benchmarks have climbed in successive cycles rather than holding steady.

Cost-Per-Hire by Role Level, From $1,500 for Entry-Level to $35,879 for Executives

A single cross-industry average masks a cost-per-hire range that stretches from roughly $1,500 for entry-level positions to $35,879 for executive hires. That 20x spread makes role-level segmentation essential for budgeting, and no public benchmarking resource currently provides it.

Entry-level and high-volume hourly roles sit at the bottom of the range. Figures for frontline and beginner positions fall between $1,000 and $2,500 per hire, while entry-level professional roles commonly benchmark at $1,500-$3,500 depending on sourcing channels and interview intensity. Mid-level professional hiring climbs to $3,500-$8,000, and technology or engineering hires, the most expensive individual-contributor category, routinely reach $6,000-$23,000 because of competitive market conditions and longer technical interview panels. Senior management fills push costs into the $10,000-$30,000 band.

Executive hiring stands apart. SHRM’s 2025 benchmark puts the average executive cost-per-hire at $35,879, up 21% from 2022 and nearly 7x the non-executive average 1. Earlier estimates landed around $28,329, illustrating how the figure has climbed across reporting cycles. The gap reflects far more than longer processes; it is driven by the increased sourcing complexity, multiple stakeholder interviews, and heavier reliance on external search firms that characterize senior-level recruitment.

Recruitment fees scale directly with role level and account for a large share of that escalation. Contingency search fees typically range from 15% to 25% of first-year salary. At the entry level, many organizations avoid agencies altogether to control costs, but where they are used, for instance, at a 20% rate on a $30,000 salary, the absolute fee still reaches $6,000. Mid-level roles are filled via agencies more often, with fees in the same 15 to 25% band. For senior executives, retained search fees of 30 to 35% of salary become the norm. A single $200,000 executive placement at a 30% fee produces a $60,000 recruiting charge, a line item that can eclipse every other hiring cost combined. In sectors with acute talent shortages, such as technology, financial services, and health sciences, those fee percentages skew higher still.

These role-level benchmarks are the starting point. The next sections layer on how industry and geography further widen the cost gaps.

Industry and Geography, How Sector and Location Shape Cost-Per-Hire

Healthcare organizations routinely spend $9,000-$12,000 per hire, with credential verification, licensing checks, and reliance on specialized staffing agencies pushing costs to the top of the range. That pattern puts healthcare at two to three times the national average. Skilled trades positions cost $12,000 or more, and legal and professional-services hires run $16,000-$20,000. Technology and software development roles carry their own premium: competition for engineering talent in markets like San Francisco, Austin, Seattle, and New York drives up sourcing costs and time-to-fill. Manufacturing, by contrast, sees moderate CPH ranges for skilled trades, but geographic concentration and skilled-labor shortages in the US Midwest and Southeast are pushing those figures upward in 2025.

Location widens the divide further. Regions with a higher cost of living require more competitive salaries and benefits, which directly raises cost-per-hire. Searches based in major metropolitan areas cost more to operate, and the same high-demand tech hubs that inflate software-role costs push all-role CPH above the national figure. Together, sector and geography can more than double the cost of a hire.

The Small-Business Penalty, Why SMBs Pay 53% More Per Hire Than Enterprises

Beyond industry and geography, company size introduces another dramatic cost multiplier. Small businesses pay roughly 53% more per hire than enterprises. Company size is one of the largest drivers of cost-per-hire variation, and the direction is counterintuitive: smaller organizations routinely pay more per hire than large enterprises, despite spending less in total.

The reason is scale. Large enterprises spread fixed recruiting costs like ATS licenses, recruiter salaries, and employer branding across hundreds or thousands of hires per year. A company spending $200,000 on a two-person recruiting team will have a much higher per-hire cost than an enterprise spreading $2 million across 20 recruiters making 1,000 hires. The total spend is smaller, but the per-hire math is worse. Enterprises also fill roles as much as five days faster than their smaller counterparts, further compressing cost 3.

Small and mid-size businesses lack this leverage. They rarely maintain dedicated recruiting teams, rely more heavily on agencies or job board postings, and often lack the brand recognition needed to attract candidates quickly. When a business makes 5-10 hires per year instead of 500, each individual hiring decision carries disproportionate weight on the budget, since fixed costs cannot be amortized the way an enterprise can. This explains why cost-per-hire is the number one HR metric tracked by 47% of businesses with 5-19 employees and 54% of businesses with 20-49 employees.

The cost-per-hire benchmarks by company size bear this out. Small businesses under 100 employees typically see $3,500 to $5,500 per hire. Mid-market companies with 100-999 employees range from $2,500 to $4,500. Enterprise organizations with 1,000+ employees often achieve $1,500 to $3,500 per hire.

Those averages flatten important variation by role level. For most small businesses, roughly 80% of hires fall into the entry-level and mid-level categories, where costs are lower but still inflated by the lack of in-house recruiting infrastructure. Entry-level roles run $2,000 to $4,000, and mid-level roles such as operations or bookkeeping run $3,500 to $7,000. Senior and specialized hires such as developers, controllers, and managers push costs to $6,000-$12,000 as limited supply extends the search. Executive hires, which typically require agency or retained search, run $15,000 to $40,000 or more. To control these costs, it's essential to know how to calculate cost per hire accurately.

How to Calculate Cost Per Hire: Methodology, Standards, and Measurement

The standard defines CPH as the mean of total costs divided by the number of hires:

**Cost Per Hire = (Total Internal Costs + Total External Costs) ÷ Total Number of Hires**

Internal costs encompass recruiting staff salaries and benefits, prorated hiring-manager interview time, HR administrative time, internal referral bonuses, and recruiting technology subscriptions. External costs include job board postings, agency fees, advertising, background checks, drug testing, skills assessments, and candidate travel expenses.

Measurement discipline matters as much as the formula. The hardest input to estimate is salary allocation: splitting a recruiter’s time across hiring, operations, and employer branding. SHRM expects high data quality, not a penny-by-penny reconciliation, and acknowledges that some inputs will be estimates. Two common errors undermine comparability: omitting hiring-manager time entirely and mixing onboarding costs with recruitment costs, which makes it impossible to benchmark against industry standards. Data should be gathered for a consistent period (quarterly is the most practical cadence) and costs must align with hires from the same period.

A worked example: a mid-sized company makes 15 hires in Q1. Internal costs total $30,000 (recruiter salaries, HR admin time, ATS subscription, hiring-manager time, and referral bonuses). External costs total $25,000 (job board ads, agency fees, background checks, and assessment fees). CPH = ($30,000 + $25,000) ÷ 15 = $3,667. However, that standard calculation leaves out substantial soft costs, as the next section explains.

Hard Costs vs Soft Costs: Why Reported Cost Per Hire Is 2-3x Too Low

The SHRM benchmark of roughly $5,475 per hire captures mostly direct costs; once indirect costs are added, the real cost per hire often runs two to three times higher. “Most companies don’t actually know what hiring costs them. They only know what they can see on the invoice”. The updated SHRM figure itself is characterized as only capturing what shows up on an invoice, not the fuller cost.

Direct hiring costs are the expenses you can point to on a receipt: job ads, agency fees, background checks, assessment tools, signing bonuses, and ATS subscriptions. Indirect costs, often called soft costs, are the expenses of recruitment that never appear on an invoice, tied to time, productivity, and risk. They are harder to measure and easy to underestimate. Industry estimates place indirect costs at roughly 60 to 70% of the total cost of hiring. Hard costs are the smaller fraction (approximately 30 to 40% of the total), while soft costs account for the larger share.

Where do these invisible costs come from? A substantial share arises from the hours internal staff spend on the hiring process rather than revenue-generating work: writing job descriptions, reviewing resumes, conducting interviews, and scoring candidates. If an in-house Talent Acquisition specialist earns $60,000-$80,000 per year and handles approximately 40 hires annually, that works out to roughly $2,000 per hire in salary cost alone, before counting the time of hiring managers and interview panel members. Each additional interview round multiplies this further, since panels typically involve two or more managers or team members whose time must also be costed in. Beyond recruiter and interviewer time, indirect costs include lost productivity during the vacancy period, slower onboarding, administrative processing, and the productivity ramp while a new hire gets up to speed.

Because hard costs are visible on a ledger and soft costs are diffused across many people’s time, organizations that track only invoiced spending are managing the smaller half of their hiring budget while flying blind on the larger half. Understanding that direct spend represents only part of the picture is essential before examining what the standard cost-per-hire formula leaves out.

What the Standard Formula Captures, And What It Leaves Out

Cost-per-hire (CPH) tallies the total expense of filling a role, split into external costs (job advertising, background screening, agency fees, candidate travel) and internal costs such as recruiter salaries, interviewing time, and employee referral bonuses. The SHRM/ANSI standard formula captures these direct, invoice-visible expenditures, but it structurally omits the indirect costs that often make the total two to three times higher: recruiter and hiring-manager hours, lost productivity while a role sits empty, and the expense of replacing a mis-hire.

The most visible direct external line item is job-board and advertising spend. A single posting on Indeed carries a $150 minimum; LinkedIn ads run roughly $5-$9 per click. In competitive markets, monthly ad spend for one role can reach $500-$2,000, and multinational firms often invest further in localized boards and region-specific campaigns. Costs vary by level: entry-level roles rely on broad postings, while executive searches frequently skip public ads entirely, outsourcing discovery to a retained recruiter whose fee absorbs the “finding” work. Job boards typically require 50 to 60 applicants per hire, and because many platform fees are annual, they must be divided by the applicable period to yield an accurate per-hire figure 2. Tracking CPH by channel, not just aggregate, is essential; a channel that appears expensive may produce faster, higher-quality hires, while a cheap channel can inflate true cost if it floods the funnel with low-fit applicants. Yet the single largest excluded cost is vacancy time itself.

Vacancy Cost, The $4,000-$9,000 Monthly Expense the SHRM Formula Excludes

The SHRM/ANSI cost-per-hire standard excludes vacancy cost: the productivity lost while a role sits empty. SHRM data separately estimates every open position costs between $4,000 and $9,000 per month in lost output, overtime, and burnout.

For an average professional role, lost output runs roughly $500 per day. With the median time-to-fill of 63.5 calendar days, roughly 44 working days, vacancy cost alone reaches $22,000 before any recruiting expense is incurred. Revenue-generating roles multiply the figure: sales roles forfeit $7,000 to $10,000 per month in lost deals, and a senior sales vacancy at $2,500 per day in lost pipeline accumulates to $112,500 over 45 days.

### What Drives Vacancy Cost Higher or Lower

Vacancy cost depends on seniority, role function, industry, and specialization. Seniority is not the only driver; the revenue or operational role matters as much. Specialized or niche roles multiply cost through longer fill times, while high-volume roles generate large aggregate vacancy cost even though each individual vacancy has a smaller per-position impact. Time-to-fill lengthens sharply with seniority: nearly 40% of senior-level roles take more than 90 days, and 44% of mid-level roles fall in the 31 to 60-day window, though 17% still stretch past 90. Across all roles, a quarter of searches report 90-plus days.

### The Hidden Costs Beyond Salary

Vacancy costs extend well past the salary line item. Unfilled roles carry a direct productivity cost, and current employees absorb the missing workload through overtime, leading to decreased morale, reduced productivity, and lost customers. Job board spend accumulates; team burnout and retention risk build in the background. Lower productivity, missed deadlines, and decreased customer satisfaction compound quickly. Gallup reports overtime-driven burnout can cut productivity by up to 68%, and burned-out employees are 2.6 times more likely to leave. The longer a role stays vacant, the more the organization risks a self-reinforcing cycle of further vacancies 3. Disengaged employees alone cost $3,400 for every $10,000 in salary in lost productivity.

### The Special Case of Executive Vacancies

Executive vacancies introduce costs daily rate calculations cannot fully capture. Extended C-suite vacancies create strategic uncertainty and can affect investor confidence and board dynamics. With nearly 40% of senior-level roles taking more than 90 days to fill, the daily cost of an empty executive seat compounds over a much longer period.

### How Time-to-Fill Varies by Seniority and Role Type

The 2026 median time-to-fill is 63.5 days, down from 67.7 days in 2025 but still above the 2024 benchmark of approximately 54 days. Time-to-fill lengthens as seniority rises: senior-level roles see nearly 40% exceeding 90 days, mid-level roles cluster in the 31 to 60-day window but 17% push past 90, and exempt roles overall show a similar pattern with almost 40% exceeding 90 days. Nonexempt roles fill faster. Most land in 30 to 60 days, yet 25% extend beyond 90. Specialized or niche roles requiring rare skills or extensive certifications often take two to three times longer to fill than standard roles, multiplying every associated vacancy cost.

Bad Hires, The Biggest Preventable Cost, at 50 to 200% of Annual Salary

Vacancy time is costly, but a mis-hire is even more expensive. Gallup places the cost of replacing an employee at 50% to 200% of their annual salary. Applied concretely, a $50,000 entry-level position carries roughly $25,000 in replacement costs, while a $100,000 senior role can run $200,000 or more. These figures capture the full cost of rehiring plus productivity losses during the vacancy and the ramp-up of the replacement.

The multiplier scales sharply with seniority. Entry-level roles trend toward 50 to 75%, mid-level managerial and technical roles toward 100 to 150%, and C-suite or executive positions toward 200% or above. A bad hire at the management level specifically has been put at $300,000 to $500,000 in total organizational cost. At the extreme end, a failed executive hire can set an organization back 3 to 5 times the executive's annual salary once lost productivity, morale damage, and foregone strategic opportunity are factored in.

The direct invoice costs are the smaller half of the problem. For a bad hire, indirect costs (lost leadership time, team productivity drag, missed performance, delayed projects, and negative cultural impact) are typically 3 to 10 times larger than the direct costs that show up on a spreadsheet. A single mis-hire can trigger a cascade of voluntary exits among high performers. The pattern: the mis-hire underperforms, high performers absorb extra workload, morale drops, and within 3-6 months 1-2 of those high performers leave for roles with less friction. The surrounding team's productivity typically declines 15 to 25% while the situation persists.

The customer impact compounds the internal damage. An American Express survey found that one poor service experience prompts 33% of customers to consider switching providers. Harvard Business Review research suggests that 80% of customer service issues stem from employee-related challenges. A LinkedIn survey found that 85% of hiring professionals reported that a bad hire negatively affected the entire team, not just the company's balance sheet.

CareerBuilder research found 74% of employers admitted to hiring the wrong person for a role. With average direct losses of $14,900 to $17,000 per poor hiring decision, the aggregate cost across an organization compounds fast. The most effective way to reduce total recruitment cost is not to find cheaper candidates but to make better hiring decisions and keep the people already hired.

The Screen-First Fix, How Pre-Employment Assessments Prevent Mis-Hires Before the Offer

The most effective lever for eliminating mis-hire cost is structural: move the evidence checkpoint before the first interview. Unstructured interviews predict job performance at only 14% accuracy; structured assessments raise that to 54%. A candidate who performs well in a 45-minute conversation may still lack the sustained attention, numerical reasoning, or collaborative behaviors a role actually requires. An assessment catches those gaps before the offer stage, when the cost of a wrong decision is still zero.

The Screen-First Hiring Model operationalizes this principle in four steps. First, every applicant completes a role-relevant assessment, replacing the resume screen with a scored, comparable signal. Second, only the top 15 to 20 percent of completers advance: for a 50-application pool, eight to ten candidates reach interview on demonstrated ability, not resume formatting. Third, one structured, scorecard-driven interview replaces the typical three disconnected rounds, cutting five to ten days off time to fill. Fourth, the team decides with multiple signals pointing the same way. The model works best for roles with 20 or more applications where competency is the primary differentiator.

Screening costs vary by depth and role level. Basic background checks run $30 to $50 per candidate; comprehensive packages reach $100 to $200. Drug testing adds $30 to $110. Pre-employment skills assessments cost $30 to $200 per candidate screened. These figures multiply across the entire pipeline, not just the eventual hire. Entry-level hiring involves more candidates and higher total spend despite lower per-unit costs; mid-level roles may use pricier specialized assessments; executive vetting carries the highest per-candidate cost despite a smaller pool. While assessments prevent mis-hires, reducing the cost of finding good candidates also matters, and employee referrals are the most cost-effective sourcing channel.

Employee Referrals and Internal Mobility, Proven Levers That Cut Cost-Per-Hire Up to 50%

Employee referrals remain the most cost-effective lever available to talent leaders. Jobvite research finds they can reduce cost-per-hire by up to 50%, while LinkedIn data shows referred employees have a 45% higher retention rate. The conversion funnel is unusually efficient: ERIN’s 2024 analysis of over 1.1 million referrals across enterprises revealed that 1 in 10 referrals results in a hire. A rate unmatched by job boards, where 50 to 60 applicants are needed per hire 2. At JLL, 13,500-plus employees generated over 11,000 referrals and 600 offers in four months 2. At SHRM, about half of all referrals become hires, and referrals accounted for more than 10% of 2024 hiring 2.

Internal mobility and in-house recruiting deliver comparable savings. Nature’s Pride reduced cost-per-hire by roughly $1,100 by bringing recruitment fully in-house instead of using agencies 4. Hiring from within eliminates advertising and agency fees and accelerates time-to-productivity; proactive succession planning further lifts retention.

Over-reliance on agencies is a primary cost driver, as referred hires cost 40 to 60% less than agency placements: a $2,000 referral bonus beats a 20% agency fee on a $100,000 salary. Employer branding compounds these advantages. Ericsson cut its cost-per-hire by 70% through social media and employee advocacy initiatives 4. A LinkedIn study found that companies with strong employer brands achieve up to 43% lower cost per hire, because a visible, trusted brand attracts qualified candidates organically, reducing dependence on paid sourcing. Beyond referrals and branding, AI-powered tools are now delivering double-digit reductions in cost per hire.

AI Recruiting Tools Are Cutting Cost-Per-Hire 20 to 40% in 2026

AI-powered recruiting tools now cut cost-per-hire by 20 to 40% while reducing time-to-hire by 30 to 50%, according to Deloitte. The shift is already underway: AI adoption in HR reached 43% in 2025, up from 26% in 2024, and 73% of companies are investing in automation tools, per SHRM. Why Low Cost-Per-Hire Isn't the Goal, You Must Pair It with Quality Metrics

A low cost-per-hire figure paired with high turnover or a long time-to-fill often signals under-investment, not efficiency. Driving down CPH without a quality signal can quietly raise downstream replacement hiring and total labor cost. The recommended safeguard is pairing CPH with a 90-day retention rate or hiring-manager satisfaction score. The target is not the lowest number but the right number for the role’s quality requirements.

Recruitment ROI makes that trade-off explicit: **(Value gained from the hire-Cost to hire) / Cost to hire**, expressed as a percentage. The cost side can reach three to four times the position’s salary once soft costs are included. The value side relies on proxies like revenue per employee or retention. Quality of hire (a blend of first-year performance, retention past probation, and hiring-manager satisfaction) is the metric that protects ROI most. One early exit can flip a 210% return into a net loss.

These metrics must be read together; a CPH number on its own can hide the real story. Tracking cost, speed, quality, and retention as a balanced set turns measurement into a defensible budget argument, not a vanity exercise.

Appendix, Full Methodology, Supplementary Cost Data, and Recruitment Fee Structures

### Using a Cost-Per-Hire Calculator A calculator aligned with the CPHC Standard separates internal costs (recruiter time, sourcing, operational expenses) from external spending (agency commissions, job board fees) and divides the sum by the number of hires in the period. This isolates the components driving total spend.

### Executive Search Fee Structures Three models dominate. Contingency search, typical for mid-level roles, charges 15 to 25% of first-year base salary: for a $70,000 role, that is $10,500-$17,500. Retained search, standard for the C-suite, runs 25 to 35% of total first-year compensation, reaching 40 to 50% for top executive positions. Fees are usually paid in thirds: on engagement, at shortlist presentation, and on placement. Hybrid or “container” models require a smaller upfront payment (10 to 25%) with the remainder contingent on placement. Additional expenses (administrative fees, travel, and assessments) can add another 5 to 15%. The difference between a 25% contingency fee and a 33% retained fee is often negligible weighed against the cost of a failed executive search.

### Other Line Items That Factor into Cost-Per-Hire Beyond agency fees, direct costs include employee referral bonuses ($500-$5,000), candidate travel and lodging for interviews 3, and relocation assistance often ranging from $5,000 to $20,000 or more. Job fair participation (booth fees, recruiter travel, and marketing materials) is another external category, as is employer branding spend, which should be prorated annually and amortized across hires. Recruitment technology subscriptions (ATS, video interview, assessment platforms) typically add a few hundred dollars per hire. Legal fees for employment contracts and, for replacement hires, severance costs are also reported.

### International Hiring and Cost-Per-Hire When hiring across borders, the line between internal recruiting spend and ongoing employment costs shifts based on the operating model. Using an Employer of Record (EOR) moves local compliance, payroll setup, and employment contract handling into external costs that sit adjacent to but are not counted within traditional recruiting cost-per-hire. Organizations frequently undercount true international hiring expense because they omit structural setup costs that precede the first payroll.

### Temp, Contractor, and RPO Costs vs. Traditional CPH Temporary and contract workers are typically paid through an agency markup spread over the engagement, making the upfront recruiting cost appear lower than for a permanent hire because recruitment is amortized over time. Total contractor cost, however, includes onboarding, offboarding, and any conversion fee if the contractor is later hired full-time. Because of this structural difference, organizations commonly measure contingent labor through markup rates or cost-of-contract-labor metrics rather than traditional CPH. Recruitment process outsourcing (RPO) offers an alternative: predictable, fixed cost-per-hire, in contrast to agency models where per-hire costs can spike.

### Onboarding Costs Even after acceptance, indirect costs continue. HR and manager time spent on orientation, onboarding systems, and sometimes travel or welcome packages add expense. Benchmarks place average U.S. onboarding cost at approximately $1,500-$5,000 per employee. Administrative tasks (payroll setup, benefits enrollment, and equipment provisioning) further add to the total. The stakes are material: 30% of employees quit within the first 90 days, and effective onboarding can lift new-hire retention by 55%.

### Training Costs Training carries direct charges (course fees, trainer salaries, and e-learning subscriptions) and the indirect cost of a new hire who is not yet fully productive. This productivity gap lasts through the ramp-up period as the employee builds toward full effectiveness. The average U.S. company spends roughly $1,300 per employee per year on training, with the figure running several times higher for specialized or technical roles; a 2024 estimate places the training cost per employee at $874 per year.
